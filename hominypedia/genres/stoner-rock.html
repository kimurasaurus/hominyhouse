Stoner Rock

Overview

Stoner rock, also known as stoner metal or stoner doom, is a rock fusion genre that combines the weight of doom metal with psychedelic and acid rock. It emerged in the early 1990s and was pioneered foremost by Kyuss and Sleep. Where doom chases dread, stoner rock chases the groove: fat, fuzz-soaked riffs played slow, often with a hazy, sun-baked feel.

The music is typically slow- to mid-tempo, with heavily distorted, groove-laden, bass-heavy guitars, melodic vocals and a deliberately warm, "retro" production. Because it shares so much with sludge metal there is frequent crossover between the two, and bands such as Electric Wizard, High on Fire and Weedeater fuse both styles. The tone is built on fuzz guitar effects, and the tuning is usually low. The descriptor "stoner rock" may derive from Roadrunner Records' 1997 compilation Burn One Up! Music for Stoners, while "desert rock" was coined around MeteorCity Records' 1998 collection Welcome to MeteorCity.

The genre is tied to the American Southwest and especially to the Palm Desert Scene of southern California, where related bands and musicians often jammed in the desert and shared members. Cannabis culture runs through the music and its imagery, though not every band embraces the "stoner" label Queens of the Stone Age and High on Fire are among those who reject it. Its near relatives are doom, psychedelic rock, acid rock, sludge, space rock and desert rock.

Characteristics

Stoner rock is typically slow- to mid-tempo, with heavily distorted, groove-laden, bass-heavy guitars, melodic vocals and deliberately warm, vintage-sounding production. Because it shares so much with sludge metal, the two often cross over, though stoner stays more laid-back and more psychedelic. The tone is built on fuzz, and the tuning is usually low: drop D at the least, often D or C standard.

History

Stoner rock's roots are hard to pin down, but several 1960s and early-1970s records are widely credited with shaping it. Black Sabbath's Master of Reality (1971) is often cited as the first album of the genre Martin Popoff has said that when "Sweet Leaf" kicks in "one witnesses simultaneously the invention of stoner rock" while Led Zeppelin were the other band that first made a monolith of the blues riff. Blue Cheer's Vincebus Eruptum (1968) is considered a key forerunner; the Beatles' "I Want You (She's So Heavy)" (1969) is credited with laying foundations through its spiralling outro; and Sir Lord Baltimore were called "the godfathers of stoner rock" by Classic Rock. Buffalo's Volcanic Rock (1973) has been "heralded as the first great stoner rock record", and Primevil's Smokin' Bats at Campton's a "touchstone". The psychedelic, proto-metallic jamming of Cream, Deep Purple, Hawkwind, Blue Öyster Cult and the supergroup Captain Beyond fed the style as well.

The genre became a named scene in the early 1990s. Doom bands pointed the way first: Trouble brought acid rock elements into their 1990 Trouble album and 1992's Manic Frustration, and Britain's Cathedral moved toward a psychedelic, stoner sound across their first three records, culminating in The Ethereal Mirror (1993). Then, in 1992, Kyuss emerged from the Palm Desert Scene with Blues for the Red Sun, hailed by critics as "a major milestone in heavy music", and San Jose's Sleep released Sleep's Holy Mountain. The two were heralded by the metal press as leaders of the emerging stoner scene. New Jersey's Monster Magnet had preceded them with Spine of God (1991), and southern California's Fu Manchu followed with their 1994 self-titled album. San Francisco's Acid King and Britain's Acrimony released debuts in 1994, and Clutch, Sons of Otis and Corrosion of Conformity were other influences of the era.

The Palm Desert Scene itself grew out of marijuana-driven instrumental jam sessions in the desert, especially those of Yawning Man, and from parties at which Kyuss and others played through petrol generators to small crowds. Josh Homme has said that playing in the desert "was the shaping factor" for Kyuss, since there were no clubs and a band could not rely on anything but the music. The scene's musicians moved freely between bands, and in 1997 Homme began the Desert Sessions at Rancho De La Luna in Joshua Tree, a collective that recorded ten volumes between 1997 and 2003.

Kyuss broke up in 1995 after their fourth album, and its members spread into new projects. That same year Roadrunner Records issued the compilation Burn One Up! Music for Stoners, and in September 1997 Jadd Shickler and Aaron Emmel founded the online store All That's Heavy in Albuquerque, New Mexico, to sell hard-to-find Kyuss, Monster Magnet and Fu Manchu releases. That grew into MeteorCity Records, whose 1998 compilation Welcome to MeteorCity gave a first recorded outing to new bands such as Sixty Watt Shaman, Lowrider, the Atomic Bitchwax, Dozer, Goatsnake and Los Natas. MeteorCity went on to sign Palm Desert acts including Hermano and Unida along with emerging Swedish bands, and The Hidden Hand and Spirit Caravan gained popularity in the same years.

In June 2000 Queens of the Stone Age released their breakthrough album Rated R, bringing the sound toward the mainstream despite the band rejecting the label; Songs for the Deaf followed in 2002, with its single "No One Knows" reaching No. 1 on the US Modern Rock chart. Detroit's Small Stone Records built a parallel compilation culture, issuing 1970s tributes such as Right in the Nuts (2000) and the Sucking the 70's collections (2002 and 2006). In 2002 the desert-rock collective Orquesta del Desierto formed, and in 2009 Electric Wizard's Dopethrone appeared on both Decibel's and Terrorizer's lists of the decade's greatest and most important albums.

Europe developed its own strong following, to the point that some American stoner bands preferred touring there; the French-founded site MoreFuzz.net became a notable promoter of the scene. European bands again drew on Black Sabbath, Blue Cheer and psychedelia: Sweden's Graveyard, Germany's Kadavar and the largely instrumental Karma to Burn are examples, alongside the Swedish stoner wave of Lowrider and Dozer. The wider family of Kyuss descendants Unida, Slo Burn, Hermano, Mondo Generator, Fu Manchu and Brant Bjork kept the sound alive as the original catalogue grew in reputation, while Queens of the Stone Age themselves moved well beyond it.
