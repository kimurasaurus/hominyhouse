Fuzzrock

Not so much a genre as the tone that makes the genre: the fuzz pedal and the wall of sound.

At a glance
Also calledFuzz; fuzz tone
Stylistic originsBlues rock, garage rock, psychedelic rock, acid rock, hard rock
Cultural originsEarly 1960s, United States; popularised from 1965
Signature tuningDrop D down to C and B standard; fuzz loves low notes
Near relativesAcid rock, stoner rock, doom metal, sludge metal, psychedelic rock, space rock, desert rock

Overview

Fuzz is a type of distortion that clips a guitar or bass signal into a thick, saturated, sustaining tone. It is the single most recognisable ingredient of the stoner and doom family, the reason the guitar sounds less like a string and more like a wall. Fuzzrock is the informal name for music built on that tone: garage rock, acid rock, stoner rock, doom and sludge all speak it fluently.

Characteristics

A fuzzbox alters the signal until it is nearly a square wave, adding complex overtones and sustain. Players lean on simple power chords and single notes, because heavy fuzz turns even one note into a thick, saturated wall of sound. These early pedals built the tone:

History

Fuzz was discovered by accident. Around 1961 a faulty preamplifier produced a distorted six-string bass tone on Marty Robbins' "Don't Worry" (played by guitarist Grady Martin). The recording engineer Glenn Snoddy and the radio engineer Revis V. Hobbs built a stand-alone device to reproduce the effect, and sold the circuit to Gibson, who introduced it in 1962 as the Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone, one of the first commercially successful mass-produced guitar pedals. The Kinks' "You Really Got Me" (1964) hacked its distortion from slashed speaker cones, and in May 1965 Keith Richards used a Maestro FZ-1 to record the Rolling Stones' "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction"; its success sent sales soaring, and all available stock sold out by the end of that year.
