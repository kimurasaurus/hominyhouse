Psychedelic Rock & Metal

At a glance
Also calledPsychedelic rock; psych-rock; "heavy psych" for the loud wing
Stylistic originsRock, blues rock, folk rock, garage rock, jazz, Indian classical
Cultural originsMid-1960s, US & UK; heavy psych from the late 1960s
Signature tuningStandard E and drop D; heavy psych often down to C or D standard
Near relativesAcid rock, space rock, stoner rock, progressive rock, occult rock, heavy psych

Overview

Psychedelic rock is a rock genre that arose in the mid-1960s among musicians aiming to recreate altered states of consciousness in sound: spacey, effects-heavy, improvisational, often surreal. The word "psychedelic" was coined in 1956 by the psychiatrist Humphry Osmond as an alternative name for hallucinogenic drugs, and the music borrowed its methods: novelty studio tricks, electronic and non-Western instrumentation, disjunctive song structures and long instrumental passages. Groups differed widely in style, and the label was applied loosely and inconsistently.

The genre's peak years were 1967 to 1969, when the 1967 Summer of Love and the 1969 Woodstock Festival carried it into an international counterculture. It bridged the passage from early blues- and folk-based rock to progressive rock and hard rock, and so contributed to the development of heavy metal.

Its heavier, louder wing (sometimes called heavy psych) pushes the same ideas through fuzz and volume, and is one of the two stated origins of stoner rock (the other being doom metal). Closest to it are acid rock, the harder American West Coast variant, and space rock.

Characteristics

Heavy psych (or "heavy psychedelic") is the point where psychedelia gets the distortion, tempo and weight of hard rock and early metal. It blends long-form jamming with fuzz-drenched riffs and often lingers in the same territory as acid rock and stoner rock. It is the direct ancestor of the desert and stoner scenes of the 1990s and of today's retro-psych revival.

History

The word "psychedelic" was coined in 1956 by the psychiatrist Humphry Osmond in a letter to Aldous Huxley. The New York folk group the Holy Modal Rounders used it on their version of Lead Belly's "Hesitation Blues" in 1964, and in January 1966 the Texan band the 13th Floor Elevators put the phrase "psychedelic rock" on their business cards. Their debut album, The Psychedelic Sounds of the 13th Floor Elevators, released that October, was the first rock album to use the adjective in its title.

The genre grew from folk rock and garage rock in the US and from beat and mod music in the UK. Bob Dylan's mid-1960s lyrics and the Indian classical music of Ravi Shankar were both important sources. The Beatles' "Norwegian Wood", on Rubber Soul (December 1965), was the first released recording by a Western rock group to feature the sitar and helped set off the raga rock trend. Early in 1966 two singles later recognised as the first psychedelic hits appeared: the Yardbirds' "Shapes of Things" and the Byrds' "Eight Miles High", the latter channelling free jazz and Indian raga through Roger McGuinn's twelve-string guitar.

Through 1966 the sound moved into the mainstream. Donovan's "Sunshine Superman" (July) became one of the first psychedelic rock singles to top the US charts, while the Beach Boys' Pet Sounds (May) and the Beatles' Revolver (August) brought studio experimentation backwards tapes, varispeed, tape loops to a mass audience. Revolver's "Tomorrow Never Knows" is often cited as the track that opened the doors to psychedelic rock.

In the United States the movement centred on San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury district. From 1965 basement shows organised by Chet Helms of the Family Dog, and clubs such as the Matrix, gave a stage to the Grateful Dead, Jefferson Airplane, Country Joe and the Fish, Quicksilver Messenger Service, Moby Grape and Big Brother and the Holding Company. According to one account the Grateful Dead "invented" acid rock at the second Acid Test, held by Ken Kesey in San Jose on 4 December 1965. Jefferson Airplane's Surrealistic Pillow (February 1967) was among the first San Francisco albums to sell widely.

British psychedelia was often more whimsical and artful, and stuck closer to pop song structures. Its London venues the UFO Club, Middle Earth and the Roundhouse paired bands with liquid light shows. Pink Floyd's "Arnold Layne" (March 1967) and "See Emily Play" (June 1967), both written by Syd Barrett, set the pattern for British pop-psychedelia, and the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (May 1967) carried studio experimentation to the top of the charts.

The genre's commercial peak ran from 1967 to 1969. The 1967 Summer of Love, prefaced by the Human Be-In that January and capped by the Monterey Pop Festival in June, made stars of Janis Joplin, Jimi Hendrix and the Who, while the Doors' self-titled debut (January 1967) brought a darker, improvisational strain. By 1968–69 the heavier acts Hendrix, Cream, Iron Butterfly, Blue Cheer and Vanilla Fudge had turned the style into hard, riff-based music. Blue Cheer's Vincebus Eruptum and Iron Butterfly's "In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida" (both 1968) are touchstones; critics later labelled this bridge between acid rock, hard rock and early heavy metal as "heavy psych". That heaviness fed directly into heavy metal, progressive rock and space rock.

Although the US and UK were the main centres, scenes developed across the world in the late 1960s: the Netherlands (the Outsiders), Denmark (Steppeulvene), Turkey's Anatolian rock (Erkin Koray, Cem Karaca, Moğollar and Barış Manço), Brazil's Tropicália movement (Caetano Veloso, Gilberto Gil, Os Mutantes and Gal Costa), Cambodia (Sinn Sisamouth and Ros Serey Sothea) and South Korea (Shin Jung-hyeon and Sanulrim).

Psychedelic rock was in retreat by the end of the 1960s. LSD had been made illegal in the United Kingdom in September 1966 and in California the following month, and was outlawed across the US by 1967; the 1969 Manson murders and the violence at the Altamont Free Concert contributed to an anti-hippie backlash. The 1969 Woodstock Festival was the style's last great gathering, and the early deaths of Hendrix (September 1970), Janis Joplin (October 1970) and Jim Morrison (July 1971) closed the era. Surviving acts moved on to roots rock, to the wider experiments of progressive rock, or to riff-based heavy rock; bands such as Black Sabbath, Deep Purple and Led Zeppelin took the heavy end toward heavy metal.

A neo-psychedelic revival in the 1980s carried the style forward: neo-psychedelia emerged in late-1970s post-punk circles through acts such as the Soft Boys and Echo & the Bunnymen, the early-1980s Paisley Underground grew up around Los Angeles (the Three O'Clock, Dream Syndicate, the Bangles and Rain Parade), and late in the decade the Manchester scene and Primal Scream's Screamadelica (1991) fused psychedelia with dance music. In the 1990s the Californian bands Kyuss and Sleep combined psychedelic rock with doom metal to create stoner rock.

A full heavy-psych resurgence followed in the 2000s, with bands such as Dead Meadow, Comets on Fire, Earthless and King Buffalo carrying the style straight back into the stoner world. Modern festivals such as Austin Psych Fest, founded in 2008, and Britain's Liverpool Psych Fest and California's Desert Daze have kept both the classic and the heavy ends of the genre in view.
