Spacerock

Overview

Spacerock (space rock) is a subgenre of rock music, loosely a form of psychedelic rock, built on long, hypnotic, atmospheric pieces with a science-fiction feel. Rather than chase a chorus, it drifts: repetition, echo and synthesisers create a sense of floating through space. Its song structures are loose and lengthy, and its centre of gravity is instrumental texture rather than the hook.

The style often uses distorted, reverberation-laden guitars, minimal drumming, languid vocals, synthesizers and lyrics about outer space and science fiction. Because it lives on texture, it can be approached either as dreamy, ambient music or as hard, distorted rock; the louder wing overlaps heavily with stoner rock and the instrumental jam scenes.

Space rock emerged in the late 1960s and early 1970s and has resurfaced repeatedly since first through the West German "cosmic music" of the early 1970s, then through the drone-heavy revival of the late 1980s, and again in today's heavy psych and stoner world.

Characteristics

Space rock favours long instrumental passages over conventional song structure, with heavy use of synthesisers, echo, reverb and delay. Rhythm tends to repeat and evolve rather than resolve, producing a trance-like effect. Lyrics and imagery borrow from astronomy, space travel and science fiction. Because the style lives or dies on texture, bands often keep guitars in standard tuning and let effects, not down-tuning, carry the weight.

History

Humanity's entry into outer space gave rock and roll its subject matter from the mid-1950s, and inspired new sounds to match. A prominent early example is I Hear a New World (1959), a concept album by the British producer Joe Meek about humanity's first encounter with alien life. Meek followed it in 1961 with "Telstar", a UK and US number one named for the newly launched communications satellite and built around the clavioline, an electronic forerunner of the synthesizer.

The genre proper grew out of the late-1960s psychedelic scene. Early Pink Floyd set the template: "Astronomy Domine", "Pow R. Toc H." and "Interstellar Overdrive", on their 1967 debut The Piper at the Gates of Dawn, framed Syd Barrett's free-form improvisations, and their second album A Saucerful of Secrets (1968) added the explicitly science-fictional "Let There Be More Light" and "Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun".

Jimi Hendrix was another early innovator, with tracks such as "Third Stone from the Sun" and "1983... (A Merman I Should Turn to Be)". London's Hawkwind, formed in 1969, fused the sound with hard rock, volume and science fiction and helped popularise the term "space rock". Gong and early Amon Düül II pushed it further out, and the German scene absorbed it in its own direction.

In the early 1970s West Germany's kosmische Musik ("cosmic music") scene explored "spacy", ambient instrumental soundscapes; the term is often used as a synonym for krautrock, and its artists Ash Ra Tempel, Tangerine Dream, Popol Vuh and Klaus Schulze drew on synthesizers and space imagery. In early 1971 Pink Floyd wrote "Echoes" for the album Meddle, a long piece about two planets meeting in space. A landmark of the genre was Hawkwind's Space Ritual (1973), a two-disc live album documenting their 1972 tour and its liquid light show, lasers and sci-fi imagery; the author Michael Moorcock wrote the lyrics for many of its spoken-word sections.

On the European mainland, the Hungarian band Omega released Time Robber (1976), Skyrover (1978) and Gammapolis (1979), while progressive groups such as Eloy and Nektar worked the same territory Nektar releasing Journey to the Centre of the Eye in 1971. The French band the Rockets, formed in 1974, moved to Italy in 1978.

In the 1980s Spacemen 3 rebuilt the style around drone and repetition, and the late-1980s and early-1990s revival extended from shoegaze, dream pop and the first wave of post-rock in the UK and the US. It took in Spacemen 3, Spectrum, Spiritualized, the Verve, My Bloody Valentine, Flying Saucer Attack, Failure, Duster and Ozric Tentacles. A 1990s revival brought the sound back to a wider audience, with Monster Magnet, Spiritualized and Flying Saucer Attack among them, and its textures carried into 2000s post-metal and drone.

In the mid-1990s a number of American bands built on the space rock of Hawkwind and Gong; some were signed to Cleopatra Records, which released numerous space rock compilations. From 1997 Daevid Allen of Gong, with members of Hawkwind, Clearlight and Blue Öyster Cult, performed with the studio project Spirits Burning. The Strange Daze festivals ran from 1997 to 2001, and a Michigan scene gathered around Burnt Hair and Darla Records and bands such as Windy & Carl, Mahogany and Auburn Lull. Today the style overlaps heavily with stoner rock and the instrumental jam scenes.
