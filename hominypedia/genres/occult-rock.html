Overview

Occult rock is rock music whose lyrics, imagery and atmosphere centre on the occult: witchcraft, the esoteric, the ritualistic and the satanic. It is also called doom rock or witch rock. It first appeared at the end of the 1960s and returned as a distinct retro-rock movement from the late 2000s.

Its sound draws on hard rock, proto-metal, psychedelic rock, progressive rock and blues, and its words commonly reference the occult, taking in influences from classic horror films to the novels of Dennis Wheatley. Despite the reputation the name suggests, the genre is not by nature gloomy or dark, though many of its bands have written music that is.

It overlaps heavily with doom and stoner rock, sharing their riffs and their sense of dread, and the early occult rock bands are widely credited with shaping those styles. The term is used both for the first wave at the turn of the 1970s and for the retro-minded revival that followed from the late 2000s.

Characteristics

  • Occult, pagan or satanic lyrical themes and stage imagery.
  • Heavy, retro-styled production recalling 1970s hard rock.
  • Organ, effects and doom tempos alongside blues-based riffs.
  • Strong overlap with psychedelia and horror soundtracks.

History

Occult rock emerged in the 1960s from groups such as Jacula, England's Black Widow and, most notably, America's Coven. A key forerunner was Iron Butterfly, whose 1968 track "In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida" was deeply influential on the shape the new sound would take. Coven's debut album, Witchcraft Destroys Minds & Reaps Souls, appeared in 1969 and is often cited as the first occult rock record; Black Widow's debut, Sacrifice, followed in 1970.

Most influentially, Black Sabbath made darkness itself the subject of heavy music when their eponymous debut arrived in 1970; reviewing it that September, Rolling Stone's Lester Bangs called it "England's answer to Coven". Other early bands, among them Blue Cheer, Hawkwind and Blue Öyster Cult, are counted alongside Coven and Black Sabbath as formative influences on heavy metal and on its heavier offshoots such as doom metal, stoner rock and sludge metal. Blue Öyster Cult's own debut album appeared in 1972 and was a particular influence on later bands such as Ghost.

Occult rock's association with the dark and the diabolic had a cost. Around the time of the Manson Family murders, many occult rock albums were halted from distribution, a move that did as much to feed public fear of the music as to suppress it. The imagery survived regardless and was taken up again by the new wave of British heavy metal in the late 1970s and early 1980s, through bands such as Venom, Satan and Hell.

Blue Öyster Cult also carried the style toward the mainstream in the mid-1970s: their fourth album, Agents of Fortune, arrived in 1976, and its first single, "(Don't Fear) The Reaper", reached number 12 on the Billboard Hot 100.

A retro-occult wave emerged in the late 2000s and 2010s, its figureheads including Ghost, The Devil's Blood, Blood Ceremony, Witch Mountain, Luciferian Light Orchestra, Orchid, Jex Thoth, Uncle Acid & the Deadbeats, Jess and the Ancient Ones, The Oath and Witchcraft, leaning on vintage gear, analogue recording and theatrical presentation. Prog magazine described the revival as spanning post-rock, psych-prog and progressive rock, with a marked number of bands fronted by women. Ghost's third and fourth albums, Meliora and Prequelle, reached numbers 8 and 3 on the Billboard 200, taking the sound to arena scale.

Coven and Black Widow reformed in 2007 and toured with the bands they had influenced. In Germany, Kadavar were seen by some critics as spearheading a national occult rock scene, though the band rejected the label themselves; their third album, Berlin, reached number 21 on the Billboard Heatseekers chart in 2015.
