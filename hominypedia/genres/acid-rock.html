Acid Rock

At a glance

Signature tuningStandard E (1960s); drop D common in later revivals

Overview

Acid rock is a loosely defined type of rock music that evolved out of the mid-1960s garage punk movement. It is a subgenre or sibling of psychedelic rock, generally heavier, louder and more improvisational than the rest of the movement. The name is sometimes used interchangeably with psychedelic rock, but it usually signals harder guitar, more distortion and a greater debt to the blues: heavy, distorted guitars, wah-wah and fuzztone pedals, cryptic drug references and long improvised jams.

Named after LSD, acid rock developed mainly from the American West Coast, where groups set aside the novelty recording effects of British psychedelia and the folk-rock leanings of San Francisco in favour of a rawer, harder sound built on West Coast blues rock. It overlaps heavily with 1960s garage rock, proto-metal and early hard rock. The percussionist John Beck went as far as to define "acid rock" as synonymous with hard rock and heavy metal, and critics later labelled the bridge between acid rock, hard rock and early heavy metal as "heavy psych".

As the movement ran into the late 1960s and 1970s, its elements split into two directions hard rock and heavy metal on one side, progressive rock on the other and these are the ingredients that later hardened into doom and stoner rock.

Characteristics

  • Heavy, distorted, blues-based electric guitar played loud.
  • Long, improvisational tracks, sometimes filling a whole record side.
  • Feedback and volume used as texture rather than accident.
  • Psychedelic imagery and lyrics about altered perception.

History

The label dates from the late 1960s and was applied to bands whose music was bound up with the era's drug culture and whose live shows leaned on volume and free-form jamming. The term was first used for the background music of LSD "acid trips" at underground parties such as Ken Kesey's Acid Tests, and as a catch-all for the eclectic Haight-Ashbury bands of San Francisco. Unlike the more song-oriented psychedelic pop, acid rock put the groove and the riff first.

The style's roots lay in the early-1960s garage rock movement, in which untrained teenagers leaned on fuzztone, wah-wah and simple riffs. American garage bands kept that rawness and energy while adding heavy distortion, feedback and layered effects. The Count Five's "Psychotic Reaction" (1966) was among the earliest successful acid rock songs, and the 13th Floor Elevators formed in Austin, Texas, in late 1965 moved from garage rock to become one of the original acid rock bands, their debut The Psychedelic Sounds of the 13th Floor Elevators (1966) containing the garage hit "You're Gonna Miss Me". The Paul Butterfield Blues Band's 1966 instrumental "East-West" has been described as laying "the roots of psychedelic acid rock".

The Beatles' Revolver (1966), especially "Tomorrow Never Knows", and their June 1967 album Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band were major influences on American acid rock groups. The Beach Boys' "Good Vibrations" (1966), with its electro-theremin, also sent many musicians back to the studio. As the psychedelic scene gathered, the West Coast produced a rawer, harder sound than Britain's more whimsical variant: acid rock was characterised by long guitar solos, electronic organs, deliberate distortion and feedback, and lyrics that referred to drugs, as in Jefferson Airplane's "White Rabbit" (1967) and the Jimi Hendrix Experience's "Purple Haze" (1967).

In the Bay Area the movement centred on the Haight-Ashbury. The Charlatans were among the first San Francisco acid rock bands, and Jefferson Airplane was the first of them to sign to a major label and reach the mainstream. By July 1967 Time magazine could write of the "turned-on sound of acid-rock groups", naming Jefferson Airplane, the Doors and Moby Grape, and in 1968 Life called the Doors the "kings of acid rock". The San Francisco Sound around these bands mixed almost free-jazz-like improvisation with distorted guitars, repetitive drum beats and experimental tape effects.

By the early 1970s acid rock's heavier wing fed directly into hard rock and heavy metal, and its darkness into doom. Heavy metal evolved from psychedelic music and acid rock; the heavy, blues-influenced psychedelic hard rock of the Jimi Hendrix Experience, Deep Purple and Cream was itself classified as acid rock, while Blue Cheer, Iron Butterfly and Vanilla Fudge became examples of early heavy metal or proto-metal. Iron Butterfly's "In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida" (1968) is often described as the point where acid rock turned into heavy metal, and Blue Cheer's Vincebus Eruptum (1968) is named alongside it. Steppenwolf's "Born to Be Wild" (1968) was used in the 1969 film Easy Rider and lent the genre a lasting anthem.

In the 1970s the elements of psychedelic music split into two directions: the hard rock and heavy metal of Black Sabbath, Deep Purple and Led Zeppelin on one side, and the progressive rock of Pink Floyd, Yes, King Crimson and Emerson, Lake & Palmer on the other. Bands such as Yes and Pink Floyd kept the psychedelic movement alive for a time before moving toward electronic and classical experiments. In the 1990s the stoner metal genre combined acid rock with other hard rock styles such as grunge and doom metal, updating the heavy riffs and long jams.

Why it matters here

Acid rock is named as a stylistic origin of stoner rock in its own right. Black Sabbath's early albums grew from exactly this mix of blues, acid-rock heaviness and dread, and Trouble later carried acid rock explicitly into doom.
