Progressive Metal & Prog Rock

Rock and metal with ambition: length, structure and technique, and the seed of progressive doom.

At a glance
Also calledProg, prog rock, art rock, symphonic rock
Stylistic originsRock, classical, jazz, folk, psychedelic rock, heavy metal
Cultural originsMid to late 1960s, United States and United Kingdom; progressive metal from the mid-1980s, US
Signature tuningStandard E and drop D; 7-string B standard; often drop C in prog-metal
Near relativesPost-metal, space rock, occult rock, psychedelic rock, progressive metal, progressive doom, art rock

Overview

Progressive rock ("prog rock") is a broad subgenre of rock that grew out of late-1960s psychedelia and peaked in the early-to-mid 1970s. It favours long, complex compositions, concept albums and a blending of rock with classical, jazz and folk. Progressive metal then applied the same ambition to heavy metal from the mid-1980s. In the stoner/doom world, prog supplies the long-form structure and dynamics behind progressive doom.

Characteristics

  • Extended pieces, multi-part suites and whole-album concepts.
  • Unusual time signatures and shifting metres.
  • Synthesisers, Mellotron, strings and orchestral colour.
  • High instrumental technique and long solo sections.

History

Prog rock coalesced in late-1960s Britain around King Crimson (In the Court of the Crimson King, 1969), Yes, Genesis, Jethro Tull, Emerson, Lake & Palmer and Pink Floyd. It peaked commercially in the early 1970s and spread to America with Rush and Kansas. Punk and disco pushed it aside in the late 1970s, but it returned repeatedly, feeding progressive metal (Rush, Queensrÿche, Fates Warning, Dream Theater) and later progressive death and post-metal.

Progressive doom applies prog's structure and ambition to doom metal's tempo and weight. Oregon's YOB is a standard-bearer, alongside bands such as Pallbearer and, in adjacent territory, the post-metal of Neurosis, Isis and Cult of Luna.
