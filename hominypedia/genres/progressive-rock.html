Progressive Metal & Prog Rock

At a glance
Also calledProg, prog rock, art rock, symphonic rock
Stylistic originsRock, classical, jazz, folk, psychedelic rock, heavy metal
Cultural originsMid to late 1960s, United States and United Kingdom; progressive metal from the mid-1980s, US
Signature tuningStandard E and drop D; 7-string B standard; often drop C in prog-metal
Near relativesPost-metal, space rock, occult rock, psychedelic rock, progressive metal, progressive doom, art rock

Overview

Progressive rock ("prog rock", or simply "prog") is a broad subgenre of rock that developed in the United States and United Kingdom through the mid- to late 1960s, emerging from psychedelia and peaking commercially in the early-to-mid 1970s. It favours long, complex compositions, multi-part suites, concept albums and a blending of rock with classical, jazz and folk, and it is associated with poetic lyrics, unusual time signatures and high instrumental technique.

Progressive metal then applied the same ambition to heavy metal from the mid-1980s, combining metal's distorted guitars and aggression with the more experimental, quasi-classical structures of prog rock. Its players are known for extreme technical proficiency, unorthodox harmony and complex rhythms with frequent metre changes and syncopation; the style took shape in the late 1980s and reached a wide audience in the 1990s.

In the stoner/doom world, prog supplies the long-form structure and dynamics behind progressive doom, and the style remains closely entangled with post-metal and space rock.

Characteristics

  • Extended pieces, multi-part suites and whole-album concepts.
  • Unusual time signatures and shifting metres.
  • Synthesisers, Mellotron, strings and orchestral colour.
  • High instrumental technique and long solo sections.

History

Progressive rock grew out of the mid- to late 1960s, as British and American groups began to fuse rock with classical, jazz and folk. The Beach Boys' Pet Sounds (1966) and the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967), with their lyrical unity, extended structures and studio experimentation, are widely regarded as the genre's beginnings, and the LP became a creative format as important as the single. In Britain, symphonic rock emerged from the psychedelic scene in 1967, led by the Nice, Procol Harum and the Moody Blues, whose Days of Future Passed was recorded with the London Festival Orchestra.

Almost every major prog band released its debut between 1968 and 1970, among them Jethro Tull, King Crimson, Yes, Genesis, Van der Graaf Generator, Emerson, Lake & Palmer, Gentle Giant, Barclay James Harvest and Renaissance. King Crimson's In the Court of the Crimson King (1969) and Yes' self-titled debut (1969) were early, fully formed examples. The term "progressive rock" had appeared in the liner notes of Caravan's 1968 debut, and the related Canterbury scene, built around Soft Machine and Caravan, emphasised wind instruments, complex chord changes and long improvisation.

The genre's commercial peak came in 1971–1976, when Jethro Tull, ELP, the Moody Blues, Yes and Pink Floyd between them placed five albums at number one in the United States and sixteen in the top ten. Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon (1973) became the best-selling album of the whole progressive period, and Mike Oldfield's Tubular Bells (1973), an excerpt of which opened the film The Exorcist, sold 16 million copies. The style remained largely a European phenomenon; in North America it took hybrid forms with Rush, Kansas and Todd Rundgren's Utopia, while Germany's krautrock scene (Can, Neu!, Faust) ran in parallel.

Punk and disco pushed prog aside in the late 1970s. King Crimson broke up in September 1974, and Van der Graaf Generator, Gentle Giant and U.K. dissolved between 1978 and 1980, while surviving bands simplified their sound towards arena rock and symphonic pop (Supertramp, 10cc, the Alan Parsons Project, ELO). A first wave of progressive metal followed from the American heavy metal scene of the early-to-mid 1980s: Queensrÿche and Fates Warning issued their debut albums in 1984, and Watchtower's Energetic Disassembly (1985) and Coroner's No More Color (1989) pushed the style towards jazz-fusion complexity.

Prog returned in the early 1980s through the keyboard-driven neo-prog of Marillion, IQ, Pendragon, Pallas and Twelfth Night, most of whom released debuts between 1983 and 1985. King Crimson's reunion on Discipline (1981) and Yes' 90125 (1983), with its US number-one single "Owner of a Lonely Heart", showed two very different directions. A 1990s third wave, including the Flower Kings, Spock's Beard, Glass Hammer and Porcupine Tree, revived 1970s symphonic prog, while Dream Theater's Images and Words (1992) and Awake (1994) helped institutionalise classic progressive metal alongside Queensrÿche's Operation: Mindcrime (1988) and Voivod's Nothingface (1989).

From the late 1990s, progressive rock fed into extreme metal, beginning with Sweden's Edge of Sanity (Crimson, 1996) and Opeth (Orchid, 1995; Blackwater Park, 2001), and into the 2000s "new prog" of System of a Down, Coheed and Cambria and the Mars Volta. The 2010s brought the djent strain of progressive metal, led by Meshuggah and later Periphery and Tesseract, and the Progressive Music Awards, launched in 2012 by Prog magazine. Dream Theater won their first Grammy, for Best Metal Performance, with "The Alien" on 3 April 2022.

Progressive doom applies prog's structure and ambition to doom metal's tempo and weight. Oregon's YOB is a standard-bearer, alongside bands such as Pallbearer and, in adjacent territory, the post-metal of Neurosis, Isis and Cult of Luna.
