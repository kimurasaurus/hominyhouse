Doom Metal

At a glance

Overview

Doom metal is an extreme subgenre of heavy metal built on slower tempos, low-tuned guitars and a much thicker, heavier sound than other metal. Both the music and the lyrics aim to evoke despair, dread and impending doom. It is strongly influenced by early Black Sabbath, and is the parent of stoner rock and sludge metal alike.

Its defining traits are the slow tempo itself and a bass-heavy wall of sound: guitars and bass are down-tuned and heavily distorted, often playing the same riff in unison, and the music favours minor tonality and dissonance, especially the tritone. Vocals range from the clean, mournful wails of traditional doom to the operatic singing of epic doom, with growled or screamed voices in the styles that borrow from other extreme metal. Lyrically it stays gloomy and pessimistic, dealing in suffering, depression, fear, grief, dread and death, sometimes through occult or religious imagery used purely for atmosphere.

Doom has since branched into a large family of subgenres, from traditional and epic doom to death-doom, funeral doom and drone metal, as well as its own fusions with stoner and sludge. Its derivative post metal grew out of the same slow, heavy impulse, and distinct regional scenes took shape in Finland, Louisiana, the Washington D.C. and Maryland area, and the Pacific Northwest.

Characteristics

Electric guitar, bass and drums are the core (keyboards sometimes appear), but the structures grow from the blues. Guitarists and bassists down-tune to very low notes and use heavy distortion for a thick tone, often playing the same riff in unison to build a bass-heavy wall of sound. Slow tempos, minor tonality, dissonance (especially the tritone) and repetitive rhythms are hallmarks.

Traditional doom vocalists favour clean, mournful singing (think Ozzy Osbourne's wails or Bobby Liebling's, of Pentagram), while "epic doom" leans operatic and death-doom, black-doom and funeral doom use growls and screams. Lyrically it is gloomy and pessimistic: suffering, depression, fear, grief, death and anger, sometimes with occult or Christian imagery.

History

The first traces of doom appear in the late 1960s. The Beatles' "I Want You (She's So Heavy)" (1969) is often named as an early seed, and Black Sabbath are generally regarded as the genre's progenitors. Formed in Birmingham in 1968, the band was rooted in the blues but pushed into deliberate darkness: Tony Iommi's loud, doomy guitar and the group's then-uncommon pessimistic lyrics and atmosphere set the standard for everything that followed. In the early 1970s Black Sabbath and Pentagram (whose side project Bedemon recorded the same heavy, dark music) were already working in this vein, though the term "doom metal" was not yet in use.

A wider set of proto-metal records shaped the sound. Blue Cheer's Vincebus Eruptum (1968) is often hailed as a forerunner of stoner and doom alike; Led Zeppelin's "No Quarter", Uriah Heep's 1972 Demons and Wizards, the heavy Welsh rock of Budgie, Bang's 1971 debut, Sir Lord Baltimore, Buffalo, Necromandus, Lucifer's Friend and Leaf Hound all fed into it, and Japanese psychedelic albums such as the Flower Travellin' Band's Satori were another influence.

During the first half of the 1980s the style was codified and finally acquired its name. England's Witchfinder General released their debut Death Penalty in 1982. In 1984 two American pioneers put out debuts Saint Vitus with their self-titled album on SST Records and Trouble with Psalm 9 while Cirith Ungol (formed in 1971) issued King of the Dead the same year. Pentagram's debut Relentless followed in 1985, and Sweden's Candlemass proved widely influential with Epicus Doomicus Metallicus (1986), the record from which epic doom takes its name. Some bands of the period also drew on the underground gothic rock and post-punk of the decade, sharing its dark atmosphere and lyrical themes.

The Big Four of Doom

Pentagram, Saint Vitus, Trouble and Candlemass are commonly called the Big Four of doom metal.

Fusion and extreme offshoots followed. Death-doom, blending doom's slow tempos with death metal's growls and double-kick drumming, emerged in the late 1980s under the influence of Hellhammer and Celtic Frost and was pioneered by bands such as Winter, Disembowelment, Paradise Lost, Autopsy, Anathema and My Dying Bride. Funeral doom crossed death-doom with funeral dirges at even slower tempos, with Mournful Congregation, Esoteric, Evoken, Funeral, Thergothon and Skepticism among its pioneers. In the early 1990s Earth, Boris and Sunn O))) stripped the sound back to pure drone.

Doom also grew distinct regional scenes. Finland focuses on the depressive mood of the genre, with very slow tempos and melodic tones; the scene was kick-started by Rigor Mortis (later renamed Spiritus Mortis) in 1987, and notable bands include Reverend Bizarre, Minotauri, Dolorian, Shape of Despair, Thergothon, Skepticism and Unholy. New Orleans and Louisiana are the birthplace of sludge metal, a late-1980s scene pioneered by Exhorder, who first fused doom with a punk-influenced metal sound; Eyehategod, Down, Crowbar and Acid Bath followed. In Washington D.C. and Maryland, kick-started in the early 1970s by Pentagram and the Obsessed, the "Maryland doom sound" (also the "Hellhound sound", after Hellhound Records) runs from Saint Vitus, Internal Void, Iron Man, Revelation, Wretched and Unorthodox to Evoken, Spirit Caravan, Earthride and the Hidden Hand.

The Pacific Northwest chiefly Oregon, Washington and British Columbia has hosted a growing doom, sludge and stoner scene since the 1990s, shaped by the region's place as the cradle of grunge and by a sound pioneered in part by the Melvins. Its cold, rainy, forested landscape recurs in the music's imagery, and bands often cross into drone and post-metal: Oregon's YOB, Agalloch, Witch Mountain and Red Fang, Washington's Earth and Sunn O))), and Vancouver's Anciients, Astrakhan and Aaron Turner's project Sumac. Farther south, the Palm Desert Scene in California built a desert rock and stoner metal scene of its own, with Kyuss, Queens of the Stone Age, Dali's Llama, Slo Burn and Brant Bjork.
