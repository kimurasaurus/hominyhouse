Post-Hard Rock & Metal

Overview

Post-metal (and its looser cousin post-hard rock) takes the heaviness and slowness of doom and sludge and rebuilds it around the dynamics and structure of post-rock. Instead of verse and chorus, songs swell, repeat and collapse; heaviness becomes texture rather than a constant, and atmosphere and emotion matter as much as any riff. The genre emerged in the 1990s with Neurosis and Godflesh and is also known as art metal or metalgaze.

Its roots also lie in post-hardcore, the punk offshoot that kept hardcore's aggression while favouring more complex, dynamic and atmospheric writing. Post-metal inherits that breadth: tracks are typically long and loosely structured, built from repeated themes and slow crescendos, with ambient, drone, noise and shoegaze textures between the heavy passages. Vocals, where they appear, are often screamed or growled and treated as another instrument.

Post-metal sits alongside doom, sludge, progressive rock and blackgaze in the wider extreme-metal family; many bands are entirely instrumental, and the quiet loud drone arc is often the main form.

Characteristics

  • Long tracks built on slow, patient climaxes ("quiet loud" dynamics).
  • Repetition used for trance and tension rather than hooks.
  • Ambient, drone and electronic textures between the heavy sections.
  • Harsh and clean vocals used as colours, sometimes absent entirely.

History

The groundwork for post-metal was laid in the 1980s and early 1990s by American artists who combined heavy metal and punk with an avant-garde sensibility. The Melvins' blend of doom, hardcore and experimentation (notably Bullhead, 1991), Swans, Rollins Band and Fugazi all pointed forward, while Helmet's Meantime (1992) and Betty (1994) were also significant; Tool's music was being described as post-metal as early as 1993. Most of these acts came out of hardcore punk and post-punk, and their combination of sonic violence with eclecticism made them hard to categorise.

The term "post-rock" was coined in 1994, and as that movement swelled, bands from post-hardcore and experimental backgrounds began to absorb its ambience, offbeat experimentation and downcast melody into metal. The label "post-metal" itself came into use in the 1990s and settled in the 2000s. Two 1992 records are often retrospectively named the first post-metal albums: Neurosis' Souls at Zero and Godflesh's Pure. Godflesh had already pioneered a "sluggish and tortured" industrial metal on their 1989 debut Streetcleaner, while Pure opened out into long stretches of billowing noise. Neurosis, formerly a hardcore band, folded doom metal, post-punk and industrial influences into a music of texture and dynamics.

Neurosis' fifth album Through Silver in Blood (1996) was credited by Terrorizer with "effectively inventing the post-metal genre" and has been named the best post-metal album ever made; its twelve-minute "Purify" is the record's signature track. The same era's US noisecore Botch, Kiss It Goodbye, the Dillinger Escape Plan and Coalesce fed the style by merging metallic hardcore with jazz, and the labels Relapse and Hydra Head began releasing slower, bass-heavy music closer to post-rock. Hydra Head had been founded in 1993 by Aaron Turner, who in 1997 co-founded Isis.

Isis became central to a recognisable post-metal movement. Their 2002 album Oceanic, with its multilayered washes of sound and psychedelic progressions, is regarded as a classic of the genre, and Hydra Head went on to release early records by Pelican and Jesu. Pelican are an instrumental quartet inspired by Neurosis, Godflesh and Mogwai; Jesu was formed by Justin Broadrick after Godflesh ended to explore ambient guitar compositions and shoegaze. Rosetta, from Philadelphia, added space rock and ambient elements.

As black metal spread beyond Scandinavia it joined shoegaze as a major influence. Agalloch, formed in 1995, pioneered the contrast of black-metal speed with slower, more ethereal passages, and Wolves in the Throne Room's Two Hunters (2007) combined ambience and violence. The genre also went international: Japan's Boris, formed in 1992, produced the influential Dronevil and Pink (both 2005); Sweden's Cult of Luna drew on Isis' Oceanic; Belgium's Amenra signed to Neurosis' label Neurot Recordings; and France's Year of No Light moved from sludge towards monumental instrumental compositions.

Back in the United States, Russian Circles rose to prominence with a sound also indebted to Oceanic, while True Widow offered a more restrained take rooted in indie and psychedelic rock on Circumambulation (2013). The Melvins returned to the sound they had helped inspire with Pigs of the Roman Empire (2004), a collaboration with dark ambient composer Lustmord. The genre's profile grew sharply with Deafheaven, whose second album Sunbather (2013) became one of the most celebrated releases of that year. Their fusion of black metal and shoegaze, preceded by France's Alcest, was nicknamed blackgaze and inspired a new wave including Ghost Bath, Austria's Harakiri for the Sky and Belgium's Oathbreaker.
